# Lore Content Editorial Standard

**Status:** Required for all new and substantially revised editorial content
**Owner:** Senior travel editor
**Review partner:** Cultural-safety reviewer
**Applies to:** Cities, towns, villages, neighborhoods, places, facts, culture cards,
traveler phrases, food and drink, etiquette, stories, tours, experiences, narration,
images, captions, and editorial metadata

## 1. Purpose

Lore helps a traveler understand a place, not merely collect attractions. Every
published item must be accurate, vivid, useful on the ground, respectful of the
people who live there, and honest about uncertainty. Entertainment comes from a
well-observed detail, a strong human story, or a rewarding interaction. It must
never come from exaggeration, stereotype, cultural performance, danger, or the
flattening of painful history.

This standard is a release gate, not a suggestion. An item that fails any required
gate remains a draft, even if the prose is polished.

## 2. Lore's Editorial Promise

Every Lore entry should do at least one of the following:

- Help the traveler notice something they would otherwise pass.
- Explain why a place matters and to whom.
- Make a local custom, word, dish, sound, or story easier to approach respectfully.
- Give the traveler a concrete, safe action: look, listen, taste, ask, compare, or
  reflect.
- Complicate an oversimplified story without becoming academic or evasive.

Every Lore entry must also meet these principles:

1. **Specific over sweeping.** Name the street, era, community, material, sound,
   ingredient, or custom instead of describing an entire place with an adjective.
2. **People over mythology.** Show who built, protected, changed, contested, or
   continues a tradition.
3. **Present as well as past.** A place is not a museum case. Include living culture
   and current communities where relevant.
4. **Invitation, not entitlement.** Travelers are guests. Access, photography,
   worship, ceremonies, homes, and private spaces are never assumed.
5. **Wonder without hype.** A precise surprise is stronger than "best-kept secret,"
   "must-see," or an unsupported superlative.
6. **Uncertainty in the open.** Use "about," "traditionally attributed to," or
   "accounts differ" when the evidence requires it.
7. **Useful brevity.** A tile should leave the user oriented and curious, not
   under-informed or misled.

## 3. Voice and Tile Craft

Lore's voice is a perceptive local guide: warm, concise, curious, specific, and
never showy at the subject's expense.

### 3.1 Default tile anatomy

Use this structure unless the surface requires a different field:

- **Eyebrow:** 1-4 words. A useful category, not clickbait.
- **Title or hook:** 4-12 words. One clear idea, preferably concrete.
- **Body:** 18-45 words. Explain the significance and add one memorable detail.
- **Traveler cue:** 5-20 words when useful. Give a respectful action or context.
- **Attribution:** Name the speaker, community, institution, or source type.
- **Source affordance:** At least one working source link for factual editorial.

These are readability targets, not permission to remove essential context. If a
subject cannot be handled safely in 45 words, use a carefully framed tile that
opens a sourced deep dive.

### 3.2 Style rules

- Lead with the subject, not "Did you know?"
- Prefer active voice and concrete nouns.
- Use sentence case. Avoid title case in body copy.
- Use numerals for traveler-useful measurements and dates.
- Give metric and local customary units when distance, height, or temperature
  affects a travel decision.
- Define unfamiliar words on first use.
- Keep one primary idea per tile.
- Use exclamation marks rarely and never to intensify tragedy or sacred content.
- Do not use emoji as the only category, warning, or meaning.
- Do not address a community as a monolith: avoid "the locals believe" and
  "people here always."
- Avoid generic travel cliches: "hidden gem," "off the beaten path," "step back
  in time," "melting pot," "untouched," "exotic," "authentic locals," "colorful
  characters," and "discover" when it erases people already connected to a place.

### 3.3 Concise copy patterns

**Fact**

> **[Concrete surprise]**
> In [date/era], [who] [did/created/changed what]. [One consequence or visible
> detail today].

**Culture**

> **[Word, ritual, sound, or object]**
> Among [specific community/context], this [means/functions as] [plain-language
> explanation]. It is most often encountered [where/when].

**Traveler phrase**

> **[Phrase in the language's standard script]**
> [Pronunciation or approved transliteration]
> "[Natural English meaning]" Use it [setting/register]. [Limit or caution].

**Food or drink**

> **[Dish or drink name]**
> [Core ingredients and preparation] give it [specific texture/flavor]. It is
> associated with [community/occasion/place], though versions vary. [Dietary or
> alcohol note when relevant].

**Etiquette**

> **[Situation, not command]**
> In [specific setting], [behavior] is commonly appreciated. Follow posted rules
> and the lead of your host; expectations vary by [region/generation/venue].

**Sacred place**

> **[Current preferred name]**
> This is an active [place of worship/sacred landscape/memorial]. Visitors may
> [verified access]. [Dress, photography, service, or ceremony guidance].

**Contested history**

> **[What is being interpreted or disputed]**
> [Evidence-based account]. [Community or scholar] emphasizes [material point].
> Accounts differ on [specific unresolved issue], not on [established fact].

**Explorer prompt**

> **[Notice one thing]**
> From a public, accessible position, [look/listen/compare]. What does [specific
> feature] reveal about [place/theme]?

### 3.4 Weak and strong patterns

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| "The city's most iconic hidden gem." | "Look above the doorway for [specific feature], added in [year]." |
| "Locals are obsessed with this weird snack." | "[Community or neighborhood] vendors popularized [dish]; its defining feature is [detail]." |
| "Master this phrase to sound like a local." | "This polite phrase helps when [situation]. Speakers may answer in another language." |
| "The tribe disappeared from this land." | "[Nation/community] was displaced from [place] and remains a living people today." |
| "Wheelchair friendly." | "The step-free entrance is on [street]; the venue reports an elevator as of [date]." |
| "A battle where 500 men died." | "About 500 people were killed, according to [source]; the event reshaped [specific consequence]." |

## 4. Editorial Workflow

Every content wave follows the same sequence:

1. **Commission:** Define the place, traveler need, content type, affected
   communities, and desired content balance.
2. **Research:** Gather authoritative sources before writing. Record exact claims,
   dates, names, spellings, access conditions, and publication dates.
3. **Draft:** Write to the correct tile pattern. Do not draft around an unverified
   anecdote.
4. **Fact review:** Check every externally verifiable claim against its source.
5. **Cultural-safety review:** Apply Sections 6-13 whenever identity, language,
   religion, conflict, trauma, or a living tradition is involved.
6. **Traveler review:** Confirm that advice is current, practical, lawful, and safe.
7. **Accessibility review:** Check wording, media alternatives, sensory assumptions,
   and access claims.
8. **Copy edit:** Check brevity, voice, spelling, pronunciation, capitalization,
   and duplicate ideas within the destination.
9. **Technical QA:** Confirm IDs, coordinates, links, ordering, image/audio rights,
   and rendering at large text sizes.
10. **Publish and revisit:** Store reviewer and review date. Time-sensitive content
    receives an expiry or recheck date.

AI may help find leads, organize notes, or draft alternatives. AI output is never a
source, never satisfies local-language review, and never reduces the human review
required for a sensitive subject. Every generated claim must be independently
verified.

## 5. Destination Coverage Standard

A destination is "editorially ready" only when its package is balanced. A high
number of repetitive landmarks does not equal depth.

### 5.1 Minimum package by destination size

| Content | Major city | City or town | Village or small community |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Mapped places with useful hooks | 20 | 12 | 6 |
| Sourced city facts | 8 | 6 | 4 |
| Culture entries across at least 3 registers | 8 | 5 | 3 |
| Food and non-alcoholic drink entries | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| Traveler phrases when another language is locally useful | 6 | 4 | 3 |
| Etiquette or practical context entries | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| Deep dives | 6 | 4 | 2 |
| Curated tour or coherent self-guided route | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Interactive listen/look/field-note experiences | 4 | 3 | 2 |

These are minimums, not quotas. Do not create filler to reach them. If reliable
material is limited, label the destination as developing coverage and publish a
smaller, excellent set.

### 5.2 Required balance

Each package should include, where locally relevant:

- One origin or identity story with accurate naming.
- One living-culture entry, not only historic heritage.
- One story from a community underrepresented in the conventional visitor canon.
- One ordinary-life subject such as transit, markets, work, recreation, or daily
  ritual.
- One practical traveler entry.
- One free, public experience.
- One experience that does not depend on sight alone.
- Food coverage beyond a single famous dish, including a non-alcoholic option.
- Difficult history when it materially shaped the destination.
- Rural, residential, and neighborhood context where coverage extends beyond a
  central tourist district.

Do not force identity categories into a destination where they are irrelevant or
poorly sourced. Balance means representative judgment, not tokenism.

## 6. Facts, Claims, and Numbers

### Required practice

- Verify names, dates, coordinates, measurements, rankings, records, and quotations.
- Distinguish the date something began from the date a present structure opened.
- State the geographic and time scope of rankings: "largest in the country as of
  2025," not simply "the largest."
- Attribute estimates and use a range when reputable sources disagree.
- Separate legend, oral tradition, and promotional lore from documented fact.
- Explain what a statistic measures and avoid false precision.
- Recheck time-sensitive population, access, price, opening-hour, record, and legal
  claims before each release wave.
- Use BCE/CE or the convention preferred by the cited specialist source, applied
  consistently within an entry.

### Fail conditions

- A superlative has no defined scope or current source.
- A quotation cannot be traced to a reliable text, recording, transcript, or archive.
- An anecdote appears as fact because it is widely repeated.
- A number lacks a unit, date, denominator, or meaningful context.
- Marketing copy from a venue is repeated as independent fact.
- A disputed claim is written with false certainty.

## 7. Culture and Living Communities

Culture entries should reveal how people make meaning, not assign a personality to
an entire population.

### Required practice

- Name the relevant community, neighborhood, generation, trade, or setting.
- Explain whether a custom is current, revived, historic, seasonal, diasporic, or
  ceremonial.
- Include change over time. Traditions are not frozen.
- Prefer community institutions, practitioners, and locally accountable scholarship
  as sources.
- Preserve self-identification, diacritics, and preferred capitalization.
- Use quotes only when exact, contextualized, attributed, and cleared for the
  intended use.
- Present notable people as people, not mascots for a destination.

### Fail conditions

- The entry claims all residents share a behavior, belief, accent, or value.
- Difference is described as exotic, primitive, quaint, backward, or bizarre.
- Poverty, informal work, disability, or minority identity is used as visual flavor.
- A living practice is framed only in the past tense.
- A community is discussed but never named.
- A sacred or private practice is converted into a traveler challenge.

## 8. Local Phrases and Translations

A phrase tile is practical language support, not a promise that the traveler can
perform local identity.

### Required fields

- Phrase in the locally preferred script.
- Language name and, when material, regional variety.
- Natural English meaning, not only a word-for-word gloss.
- Pronunciation or transliteration reviewed by a qualified speaker or recognized
  language authority.
- Register: formal, informal, honorific, plural, gendered, age-specific, or regional.
- Use case and any important "do not use" context.
- Source and reviewer date.

### Editorial rules

- Prefer greetings, thanks, directions, dietary needs, numbers, courtesy, and help.
- Never present phonetic English as a replacement for the original script.
- Do not simplify tones, vowel length, honorifics, or grammatical gender when doing
  so changes meaning or politeness.
- Note when another language is more common in the exact visitor setting.
- Do not imply that a nation has one language or that everyone will welcome practice.
- Treat sign languages as languages, not gesture lists. Use expert-reviewed video
  rather than text-only imitation when sign-language content is commissioned.
- Never include slurs, flirtation, bargaining pressure, or ceremonial language as a
  novelty tile.

### Fail conditions

- Machine translation is the only review.
- Script, meaning, pronunciation, and register do not agree.
- The phrase sounds natural in a dictionary but not in the stated situation.
- A minority language is labeled a dialect for political convenience rather than
  accepted linguistic usage.
- The tile encourages the user to impersonate an accent.

## 9. Food and Drink

Food coverage should connect taste to people, movement, labor, ingredients, and
occasion without policing authenticity.

### Required practice

- Describe the defining ingredients, method, texture, or serving context precisely.
- Attribute origin claims carefully; distinguish invention, popularization, and
  association.
- Name communities whose labor or migration shaped the dish when supported.
- Acknowledge meaningful regional, household, religious, or diasporic variation.
- Include useful dietary context without promising allergen safety. Use wording such
  as "often contains peanuts; ask the kitchen about cross-contact."
- Mark alcohol clearly and always include appealing non-alcoholic coverage.
- Describe a venue only from current, independent evidence and label sponsored or
  paid placement unmistakably.
- Separate editorial recommendation from coupon or deal inventory.

### Fail conditions

- "Authentic" is used as an unqualified ranking or gatekeeping claim.
- One disputed inventor or origin is presented as settled.
- A cuisine is described as strange, dirty, cheap, primitive, or adventurous.
- Health, allergy, halal, kosher, vegan, or gluten-free safety is guaranteed without
  current venue-level verification.
- Alcohol is framed as necessary to participate in local culture.
- A paid offer is disguised as editorial selection.

## 10. Etiquette and Practical Conduct

Etiquette helps a visitor reduce friction. It should not turn cultural difference
into a list of traps.

### Required practice

- Tie guidance to a setting: home, transit, market, restaurant, worship service,
  beach, queue, or business meeting.
- Distinguish law, venue rule, religious requirement, and social preference.
- Prefer "commonly appreciated" to "always" unless a rule is explicit.
- Explain the reason when it is known and useful.
- Note regional, generational, religious, and situational variation.
- Tell travelers to follow posted guidance and their host's lead.
- Recheck laws and formal restrictions against current official sources.

### Fail conditions

- The tile makes residents sound easily offended or irrational.
- A custom is universalized across a country, religion, or ethnic group.
- Legal advice relies on a travel blog or old forum post.
- The guidance tells a traveler to ignore consent, queues, dress rules, or photography
  restrictions for a better experience.

## 11. Religious, Sacred, Memorial, and Vulnerable Places

These locations receive the highest duty of care. A place can be architecturally
important and still be an active site of worship, grief, burial, ceremony, or
community life.

### Required practice

- Lead with the site's current function and preferred name.
- Verify public access, service times, dress, shoes, head coverings, photography,
  recording, gendered spaces, offerings, and closures through the site or governing
  community.
- Distinguish areas open to visitors from areas reserved for worship or ceremony.
- Use imagery approved for public viewing. Do not reproduce restricted objects,
  human remains, burial interiors, or ceremonies without explicit permission.
- Keep narration subdued and optional. Provide text so a user does not need audio in
  a quiet space.
- Disable or omit gamified prompts, celebratory badges, deals, and scanner challenges
  where they would trivialize worship, burial, trauma, or remembrance.
- Do not publish precise coordinates for vulnerable archaeological, ceremonial,
  ecological, or burial sites when visibility could enable damage.
- State when the site asks visitors not to enter, photograph, geotag, or share.

### Fail conditions

- Visitor entertainment takes priority over active worship, mourning, or ceremony.
- Access is inferred from photos, maps, or another travel app.
- A restricted practice is explained as a tourist activity.
- Human remains, sacred objects, or trauma imagery are used decoratively.
- Lore directs users onto private land or reveals a protected location.

## 12. Contested History, Conflict, and Harm

Concise writing must not become false simplicity.

### Required practice

- Establish the widely supported factual baseline first.
- Name who exercised power, who was affected, and what changed.
- Distinguish disagreement about interpretation from denial of documented harm.
- Describe slavery, colonization, displacement, genocide, segregation, war, and
  political violence directly when relevant. Do not hide agency behind passive voice.
- Use community-preferred and historically accurate terms, with a brief explanation
  when terminology itself is contested.
- Include aftermath and living memory, not only the dramatic event.
- Avoid graphic detail unless necessary to understand the history.
- Add a content note when material could be unexpectedly distressing.
- Seek specialist or community review when the subject remains politically active or
  personally consequential.

### Fail conditions

- "Both sides" framing gives unsupported denial equal weight with documented fact.
- The aggressor or governing authority disappears from the sentence.
- Casualties become spectacle, trivia, or a badge challenge.
- A monument's inscription is treated as neutral history rather than one source.
- The entry resolves an active dispute that reliable sources still describe as open.

## 13. Indigenous Names, Peoples, and Places

Indigenous peoples are contemporary sovereign peoples and communities, not only the
first chapter of a destination's history.

### Required practice

- Use the Nation's, Tribe's, or community's current preferred English name and
  capitalization. Include an endonym when the community publishes and uses it.
- Verify spelling, diacritics, pronunciation, and translation with the Nation,
  community language program, cultural office, or recognized specialist source.
- Distinguish a traditional place-name, modern official name, colonial renaming,
  and proposed restoration.
- State uncertainty when several nations have relationships to the same place or
  when a historical language form is reconstructed.
- Describe removal, land seizure, treaty history, and survival accurately when
  relevant, without implying disappearance.
- Include present-day community presence, governance, cultural work, or access rules
  when germane and publicly documented.
- Follow community guidance on stories, seasonal knowledge, images, recordings,
  sacred places, and knowledge not intended for general audiences.
- Do not turn a land acknowledgment into decoration. Connect naming to specific,
  sourced history and current people.

### Fail conditions

- A community is called extinct, vanished, or prehistoric when descendants or the
  Nation continue today.
- A name is translated through an unsourced folk etymology.
- One Nation is substituted for another because both occupied a broad region.
- Traditional knowledge is published merely because it appears elsewhere online.
- Colonial exploration language erases prior presence or ownership.

## 14. Accessibility and Inclusive Travel Information

Accessibility is both a content subject and a publishing requirement.

### Required practice

- Write image alt text that conveys editorial meaning, not "image of."
- Provide transcripts for narration and captions for meaningful video or ambient
  audio.
- Ensure an experience does not rely only on sight, color, hearing, stairs, speed,
  speech, or fine motor control without an equivalent path.
- Describe access features specifically: step-free entrance location, surface,
  gradient, elevator, seating, restroom, captioning, induction loop, quiet hours,
  service-animal policy, or sensory conditions.
- Attribute mutable access information to the venue or an accessibility authority
  and show the checked date.
- Use neutral language: "wheelchair user," "accessible restroom," and "person who
  is blind" unless an individual or community prefers identity-first language.
- Avoid "suffers from," "confined to a wheelchair," "normal people," and "despite
  their disability."
- Mention uneven surfaces, loud environments, flashing light, darkness, crowds,
  altitude, and long standing periods when material to the experience.
- Never infer accessibility from a map, photo, building age, or a single entrance.

### Fail conditions

- "Accessible" or "wheelchair friendly" appears without specific verified features.
- Audio is required but no transcript or visual equivalent exists.
- A challenge rewards a physical action without an equivalent completion route.
- An emoji, color, map pin, or photograph carries essential information alone.
- Access information has no source or review date.

## 15. Sources, Provenance, Rights, and Review Dates

### 15.1 Source hierarchy

Use the strongest source suited to the claim:

1. **Primary and governing sources:** Official records, legislation, archives,
   original maps, transcripts, the site itself, Tribal/Nation publications, community
   language programs, and direct statements from responsible institutions.
2. **Specialist sources:** Peer-reviewed scholarship, university presses, recognized
   historians or linguists, conservation records, and museum research.
3. **High-quality synthesis:** Reputable journalism, edited reference works, and
   accountable local historical organizations with cited research.
4. **Lead-only sources:** Tourism marketing, business pages, personal blogs, social
   posts, forums, crowd-edited pages, and AI output. These may identify a lead but do
   not independently settle a sensitive, disputed, safety, or superlative claim.

Wikipedia is useful for orientation, links, and appropriately licensed media. For a
material claim, follow its citation to the underlying source. Do not cite a search
results page, AI summary, or unattributed content farm.

### 15.2 Two-source rule

Require two independent reliable sources, including at least one primary or
specialist source, for:

- Contested events or disputed origin claims.
- Superlatives, records, and "first" claims.
- Claims about a living culture not issued by that community.
- Indigenous names, translations, or territorial relationships when no community
  source is available.
- Legal restrictions, public safety, or material access claims.
- Quotations without a clear original publication or recording.

Two sources repeating the same press release count as one source.

### 15.3 Required provenance record

Every published editorial item must retain:

- Stable content ID and destination/place ID.
- Claim-level source URL or archival citation.
- Source title, publisher, author when available, and publication date.
- Accessed date.
- Content language and translation reviewer when applicable.
- License or rights basis for text, image, and audio.
- Writer, fact reviewer, cultural-safety reviewer when required, and review date.
- Confidence: confirmed, qualified, disputed, or developing.
- Expiry/recheck date for mutable information.
- Notes explaining material disagreement or editorial exclusions.

The user-facing Source link must open the evidence for the displayed claim, not a
generic homepage.

### 15.4 Rights and quotation rules

- Write original summaries. Do not lightly paraphrase a single copyrighted source.
- Keep quotations short, necessary, exact, attributed, and rights-compatible.
- Record the license and required attribution for every reused image or audio asset.
- Respect cultural permissions in addition to copyright. Legally available does not
  always mean culturally appropriate to republish.
- Never use a person's portrait merely because a name matches. Confirm identity and
  image context.
- Obtain and record consent for contemporary community or user-contributed media.

## 16. Anti-Stereotype and Cultural-Safety Check

Before approval, the reviewer answers every question:

- Does the entry name a specific community or context instead of generalizing a city,
  country, ethnicity, religion, gender, age group, class, or disability?
- Is the subject given agency, or merely observed as local color?
- Would the same tone be used for a wealthy or culturally dominant community?
- Does the copy sexualize, infantilize, romanticize poverty, or aestheticize hardship?
- Does it imply that modern life makes a culture less real or authentic?
- Are humor and surprise directed at a fact or situation, not a people, accent,
  belief, food, body, or trauma?
- Does the entry distinguish public invitation from private or sacred practice?
- Are internal differences and change over time acknowledged where relevant?
- Is a community described in the present tense when it exists in the present?
- Has tourism's effect on residents, housing, ecology, or sacred space been considered?
- Would a well-informed member of the represented community recognize the entry as
  careful, even if they might choose different emphasis?

Any "no" requires revision or documented escalation to the senior editor.

## 17. Tours, Interactive Experiences, and Narration

- A tour must have a coherent idea, safe public route, realistic time, and a useful
  reason for every stop.
- Verify route continuity, crossing safety, opening constraints, surfaces, stairs,
  rest opportunities, and an accessible alternative where possible.
- Never encourage trespass, dangerous viewpoints, removal of objects, touching
  protected material, or disruption of residents and worshippers.
- Avoid clustering all stops around monumental or elite history. Include ordinary
  life, labor, nature, and living culture when relevant.
- An interactive prompt must be completable without performing a culture, disturbing
  others, purchasing alcohol, or revealing personal data.
- Narration must match the approved text, pronounce names correctly, include a
  transcript, and avoid theatrical accents.
- Use sound intentionally. Do not layer celebratory music under accounts of violence,
  burial, displacement, or disaster.
- Offer a text equivalent and respect device silence in worship, memorial, wildlife,
  and residential settings.

## 18. Pass/Fail Publication Gates

An item publishes only when every applicable gate passes.

| Gate | Pass | Automatic fail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **G1 Accuracy** | Every material claim matches current reliable evidence; uncertainty is labeled. | Unverified claim, wrong identity, invented detail, false certainty, or stale mutable fact. |
| **G2 Provenance** | Direct source, rights basis, reviewer, and review date are recorded. | Missing source, generic homepage, AI as source, or incompatible license. |
| **G3 Cultural safety** | Relevant people are named with agency; context and permissions are respected. | Stereotype, erasure, restricted knowledge, exploitative framing, or unsafe exposure. |
| **G4 Language** | Script, meaning, pronunciation, register, and qualified human review are complete. | Machine-only translation, accent imitation, or materially misleading usage. |
| **G5 Traveler safety** | Access and action are lawful, respectful, current, and specific. | Trespass, dangerous instruction, unverified access, or interference with community life. |
| **G6 Accessibility** | Media alternatives and specific sourced access context are present where applicable. | Essential content is sensory-only or an unsupported accessibility promise is made. |
| **G7 Copy quality** | One clear idea, concise Lore voice, useful detail, no cliche or hype. | Clickbait, filler, duplicate idea, vague superlative, or required context cut for length. |
| **G8 Destination balance** | The package meets the relevant minimums and represents more than tourist monuments. | Filler reaches a quota, living culture is absent, or coverage materially misrepresents the place. |
| **G9 Technical integrity** | IDs, coordinates, links, ordering, media, audio, and large-text rendering work. | Broken source, wrong map position, clipped essential text, mismatched media, or missing transcript. |

### Stop-ship issues

The following always block the entire affected content wave until corrected:

- Fabricated or AI-hallucinated facts, citations, quotations, people, places, or
  translations.
- Wrong-city or wrong-person media.
- Exposure of protected sacred, burial, archaeological, domestic-violence, wildlife,
  or other vulnerable locations.
- Cultural or religious content published against an explicit community restriction.
- Defamatory claims or private personal information.
- Directions that create a credible risk of injury, trespass, harassment, or service
  disruption.
- Systemic source or license omissions across the batch.

## 19. Final Editor Checklist

Before marking a content wave ready, confirm:

- [ ] Every destination has a balanced package or is clearly labeled developing.
- [ ] Every factual tile has a claim-matched source.
- [ ] Every phrase has qualified human language review.
- [ ] Every food and drink tile handles origin, variation, dietary context, and
      sponsorship honestly.
- [ ] Every etiquette item is setting-specific and avoids universal claims.
- [ ] Every sacred, memorial, and vulnerable place has verified access and permission
      guidance.
- [ ] Every contested-history entry distinguishes evidence, interpretation, and
      unresolved disagreement.
- [ ] Every Indigenous name and relationship follows community-preferred sources and
      present-day framing.
- [ ] Every narration, image, and interaction has an accessible alternative and rights
      record.
- [ ] Anti-stereotype questions are all answered "yes."
- [ ] Coordinates, routes, links, source affordances, and media resolve on device.
- [ ] Time-sensitive entries have a recheck date.
- [ ] A second human has reviewed every sensitive or high-impact item.

If an editor cannot verify a claim, the correct Lore voice is not cleverer copy. It
is a smaller claim, a clearly labeled tradition, or no tile at all.
